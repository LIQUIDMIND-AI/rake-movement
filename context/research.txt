In international trade, a "drift" or discrepancy between the Bill of Entry (BoE) filed by an importer at the destination port and the Supplier's Commercial Invoice generated at the time of departure (export port) is a frequent trigger for major customs disputes.While the physical cargo itself does not change mid-ocean, the HSN code can "drift" due to conflicting domestic classifications, chemical testing at the destination, or structural definition rules.A prime legal example of this occurs in the coal and chemical import sectors.The Archetypal Case: "Steam Coal" vs. "Bituminous Coal"A major real-world context for this drift can be analyzed through Indian customs disputes, such as Reliance Infrastructure Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs and the landmark CESTAT/Supreme Court rulings on coal classification.1. The HSN Drift ScenarioAt Departure (Supplier Invoice): The overseas exporter (e.g., from Indonesia or South Africa) tests the coal at the load port. They issue a commercial invoice declaring the cargo as Steam Coal under HSN 2701.19.20 based on the As-Received Basis (ARB) or Air-Dried Basis (ADB) of moisture and energy content. Under this code, the importer expects a lower customs duty rate or a specific tax exemption.At Destination (Bill of Entry): When the ship arrives, the customs authorities draw local samples and run lab tests. They apply mathematical conversions (like the Parr Formula) to strip away the moisture content completely to analyze the coal strictly on a dry basis. Customs then reclassifies the exact same cargo as Bituminous Coal under HSN 2701.12.00, claiming a higher duty rate.The importer is forced to file a Bill of Entry that disputes their own supplier’s invoice HSN code to clear the goods or face a misdeclaration penalty.Why the HSN Code "Drifts" Between DocumentsAs established in Indian tax precedent (e.g., Promostyle Exports and Joshi Steel Industries), the HSN code printed on an overseas supplier's invoice is not legally binding on local customs authorities. The drift typically happens due to three main factors:[Departure Invoice: HSN 2701.19.20] ──(Sea Voyage / Local Lab Test)──> [Bill of Entry: HSN 2701.12.00]
Origin vs. Destination Interpretation Standards: Different countries interpret the Harmonized System lines differently. A supplier classifies goods according to their country's export rules, while the importing port mandates strict adherence to its own domestic Customs Tariff Act and Chapter Notes.The "Essential Character" & Lab Testing Rule: If a shipment is a composite material or a geological commodity (like coal or chemicals), its HSN code at the destination port relies entirely on laboratory test results taken upon arrival, which can contradict the load-port certificates printed on the invoice.Intended Use vs. Inherent Nature: Suppliers often code invoices based on the commercial contract use (e.g., coal meant to produce steam). However, customs look exclusively at the geological parameters (e.g., Gross Calorific Value exceeding 5,833 kcal/kg), overriding the commercial invoice description.Consequences and Legal ResolutionWhen the HSN code on the Invoice and the Bill of Entry mismatch, it triggers severe administrative bottlenecks:Customs Hold & Seizure: The automated customs EDI system flags the mismatch, halting clearance.Amendment under Section 149: Importers must formally request a Bill of Entry amendment under Section 149 of the Customs Act, 1962 (in India), submitting technical literature, manufacturer certificates, and test reports to justify why the code differs from the invoice.Provisional Assessment: To prevent severe demurrage charges while arguing the HSN drift, importers usually pay a provisional differential duty bond to release the cargo while the legal classification dispute plays out in tax tribunals.
